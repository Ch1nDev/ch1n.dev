Essay · My Stance on AI

I use AI.
Here's how.

There's a lot of noise about AI right now — both from people who think it's the end of creativity, and people who treat it like a magic button. I don't agree with either. This page is my honest position: what I use it for, what I don't, and why.

01 · The honest truth

Yes, I use AI tools.

I'm not going to pretend otherwise. AI assistants are part of my workflow and have been for a while now. I use them for specific tasks where they genuinely save time or help me think through problems faster — not as a replacement for my own judgment, taste, or craft.

I'm a solo developer building three interconnected games, a personal portfolio, and various side projects — all at the same time. AI lets me move faster on the stuff that would otherwise slow everything to a crawl. That's a practical choice, not a philosophical one.

"Using a tool well is a skill. Using it blindly is a problem. I try to stay firmly on the right side of that line."

02 · Where I draw the line

What it does — and doesn't — touch.

There's a clear split in my mind between things that benefit from AI assistance and things that would be actively harmed by it. The lore for the Cloudlight universe, the design decisions behind each game, the voice of this website — those came from me, not a model. AI didn't invent the classified storytelling in Shockwave. It didn't decide that Explorer should feel like the Wastes seen from ground level. That's the stuff that matters.

What AI does help with is the scaffolding — the boilerplate, the first draft of a function I'll then rewrite, the quick lookup, the rubber-duck debugging session at midnight when I'd otherwise be stuck. It saves me time on things I know how to do, not things I don't know how to think about.

✓ Where I use it
Boilerplate code and repetitive structure
Debugging — explaining errors, checking logic
First drafts of UI I'll redesign myself
Researching things I'd otherwise look up manually
Thinking through architecture before I build it
Speeding up tasks I already understand well
✗ Where I don't
Game lore, world-building, or creative direction
Design decisions — layout, colour, feel
Anything that needs to sound like me
Publishing AI output without reading it myself
Skipping the learning because AI "got it"
Replacing thinking with prompting
03 · What I actually believe

My positions, plainly.

I've thought about this enough to have actual views. Here's where I land:

🧠
AI is a tool, not a collaborator
I don't think AI has taste, judgment, or intent. It predicts tokens. The creative decisions are still mine — the tool just removes friction on the execution side of things I've already figured out.
🎨
Generated art in commercial products is a hard no for me
For my games, everything visual will be human-made. I care about the craft behind the art, the intent behind each frame. AI-generated assets in a commercial release feel like a shortcut that costs too much — not financially, but in terms of what the work actually is.
⚡
Speed matters when you're building alone
Being a solo developer means every hour counts twice. AI lets me move at a pace that would otherwise be impossible — and that means I can ship things I care about instead of being buried in boilerplate forever. That's a pragmatic choice I stand by.
📖
Disclosure matters
If AI substantially contributed to something I put out into the world, I'll say so. I think the pretence that everything is purely hand-crafted when it isn't is what's actually corrosive — not the use of AI itself. Honesty is non-negotiable.
🔍
You still have to understand what it gives you
I never ship AI output I don't understand. If I ask for a function and can't explain what it does line by line, it doesn't go in. AI is a starting point, not an ending one. Blind trust in generated code is how you build things that quietly break.
04 · On creativity specifically

The creative stuff stays mine.

The thing I'm most protective of is the creative vision behind Cloudlight. The three-game shared universe, the asymmetric storytelling in Shockwave, the specific feeling I want Explorer to have when you first walk into the Wastes — none of that came from a prompt. That's years of thinking, obsessing, scrapping, and rebuilding ideas in my head before a single line of code was written.

AI didn't write this website's copy. It didn't write the lore Bible. It didn't decide what Rexux feels like at speed or why the golden age has to feel genuinely happy before everything falls apart. Those decisions matter too much to outsource.

"The vision is mine. The world is mine. The story is mine. AI helps me build faster — it doesn't get to decide what I'm building."

✦ Worth saying clearly

I think the anxiety around AI in creative fields is often pointed at the wrong thing. The issue isn't the tool — it's whether the person using it still has something genuine to say. I do. That's what I'm focused on.

05 · Where I'm watching

Things I'm still figuring out.

I'm not settled on everything. The training data question is unresolved — I think it matters that models are trained on human creative work, and I haven't landed on a fully comfortable position about what that means for how I use them. I'm watching how that conversation develops.

I'm also aware that my view could change. What AI can do is moving fast, and what feels like a reasonable use of the technology today might look different in two years. I'd rather update my position than pretend it was always obvious.

This page will change as my thinking does. That's the point of writing it down.

✦

The short version.

I use AI where it makes me faster without making my work worse. I don't use it where it would make my work less mine. The vision, the story, the aesthetic decisions — those stay human. The boilerplate can have some help.
